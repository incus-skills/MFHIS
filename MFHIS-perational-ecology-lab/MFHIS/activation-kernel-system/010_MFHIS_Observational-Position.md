MFHIS Observational Position

Every inquiry begins from somewhere.

The same observations can be arranged differently depending on where the observer stands. New observations are not required for this to occur. The observations may remain unchanged. What changes is the organization of attention.

This document begins from a simple proposition.

The world is encountered before it is explained.

Patterns are noticed before they are named.

Distinctions are experienced before they are formalized.

This is not a rejection of explanation. Explanation remains useful. Classification remains useful. Representation remains useful. They allow observations to be preserved, compared, and communicated.

Yet explanation is not the observation itself.

A representation is not the thing represented.

A classification does not create the distinctions it describes.

The position adopted here begins with observation and proceeds cautiously toward interpretation.

The purpose is not to eliminate interpretation.

The purpose is to preserve enough separation between observation and interpretation that each may remain visible.

This preservation becomes increasingly important when investigating systems that contain recursive structure. In such systems the observer, the observation, and the explanatory framework may all become entangled. Distinctions that initially appear obvious may gradually disappear beneath increasingly sophisticated descriptions.

The position adopted in this document is therefore intentionally conservative.

When uncertainty arises, priority is given to preserving distinctions rather than collapsing them.

When multiple explanations remain viable, priority is given to maintaining the conditions under which they can continue to be compared.

The concern is not merely what is believed.

The concern is how beliefs emerge, stabilize, and become reproducible.

---

The need for this distinction is not immediately obvious.

In many domains interpretation appears inseparable from observation. A scientist interprets measurements. An engineer interprets signals. A historian interprets records. The distinction can seem academic.

Yet a recurring pattern emerges when complex systems are studied over extended periods.

The observations often remain stable.

What changes are the explanations.

A set of observations may support multiple interpretations. New interpretations may emerge while the observations themselves remain unchanged. What initially appeared definitive may later appear partial. What appeared contradictory may later appear compatible.

This is not necessarily a failure of interpretation. It is often evidence that the observations contain more structure than any single explanation has yet captured.

For this reason, observations are treated here as a form of continuity.

Interpretations remain valuable. They organize, compare, predict, and communicate. They make observations usable. Yet they are allowed to remain provisional. They are not asked to carry more certainty than the observations support.

The preservation of this distinction becomes particularly important when investigating systems that exhibit recursive behavior.

In such systems explanations may themselves become part of what is being observed.

A model influences attention.

Attention influences collection.

Collection influences what patterns become visible.

The resulting feedback can be productive. It can also make it difficult to distinguish between what was observed and what was introduced by the process of observation itself.

The position adopted here therefore begins conservatively.

When faced with a choice between preserving an observation and preserving an explanation, priority is given to the observation.

When faced with a choice between extending an explanation and maintaining a distinction, priority is given to the distinction.

This is not because explanations are unimportant.

It is because explanations can be reconstructed.

Lost distinctions often cannot.
