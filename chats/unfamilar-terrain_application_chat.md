Reply 4:

The two preservations diverge.

**Lineage preservation** would keep every version, every change, every copyer’s mark.

**Reconstructive sufficiency preservation** would keep only what a future observer needs to remake the original note — not necessarily the intermediate versions.

In the copying situation, what becomes visible?

- Lineage preservation shows _what happened_.
- Reconstructive sufficiency preservation shows _what mattered_.

They are not the same because:

- Something could happen (a copyer adds a clarification) that is not necessary for reconstruction.
- Something could be necessary for reconstruction (the original phrasing of an ambiguous word) that never happened to be preserved in lineage.

If the goal is lineage, the copying process failed because intermediate versions were discarded.

If the goal is reconstructive sufficiency, the copying process may still fail — but for different reasons.

Reconstructive sufficiency requires:

- The original distinctions that could later be needed.
- Not the record of every copyer’s hand.

But without lineage, how does a future observer know which distinctions from the _final version_ are original and which are accumulated?

They cannot.

So reconstructive sufficiency without lineage is blind.

Lineage without reconstructive sufficiency is heavy but potentially usable.

The copying situation reveals:

> Lineage ensures reconstructability of _what was_.
>
> Reconstructive sufficiency ensures reconstructability of _what was necessary_.

They overlap but are not identical.

The situation becomes visible as a trade space, not a failure.
