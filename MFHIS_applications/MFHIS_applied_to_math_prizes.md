Originally, there were seven; one has been solved (the Poincaré Conjecture, solved by Grigori Perelman, who famously turned down the money), leaving six open.

## 1. Computer Science & Logic: The $P \text{ vs } NP$ Problem

This is arguably the most important open question in theoretical computer science.

- **The Concept:** $P$ represents problems that are easy for a computer to _solve_ (like sorting a list of names). $NP$ represents problems where a solution is easy to _check_ if someone gives it to you (like a complex Sudoku puzzle).
- **The Question:** Does being able to quickly verify a solution mean there is also a quick way to find it? If $P = NP$, it would mean tasks like cracking modern encryption or finding the absolute optimal route for thousands of delivery trucks could be done instantly. Most scientists suspect $P \neq NP$, but no one has proven it.

To claim the **$1 million Millennium Prize** from the Clay Mathematics Institute for the $P \text{ vs } NP$ problem, you have to do something that has stumped the world’s greatest mathematicians and computer scientists for decades.

Here is exactly what you need to provide and the process you have to follow:

---

## 1. The Mathematical Proof

You must provide a mathematically rigorous proof that settles the question once and for all. You have two choices for what to prove:

### Option A: Prove that $P = NP$

You must prove that every problem whose solution can be _verified_ quickly (in polynomial time) can also be _solved_ quickly.

- **How to do it:** The most direct way would be to find a "polynomial-time algorithm" for a known **NP-complete** problem (like the Traveling Salesperson Problem or the Boolean Satisfiability Problem). If you can create an algorithm that solves just _one_ of these problems efficiently, you automatically solve all of them.

### Option B: Prove that $P \neq NP$ (The more likely scenario)

You must prove that there exist problems whose solutions can be easily verified, but which _cannot_ be solved efficiently by any computer, no matter how clever the algorithm.

- **How to do it:** You have to mathematically demonstrate that _no possible_ polynomial-time algorithm can ever exist for an NP-complete problem.

---

## 2. The Publication Requirement

You cannot just mail your handwritten notes to the Clay Mathematics Institute. The rules are very strict about the peer-review process:

- **Scientific Journal Publication:** You must publish your complete proof in a "journal of worldwide repute."
- **The Waiting Period:** Once published, the mathematical community must be given **two years** to scrutinize your work. During this time, experts around the world will hunt for counterexamples, gaps in logic, or flaws in your math.

---

## 3. CMI Advisory Committee Verification

If your proof survives the two-year waiting period without being debunked, the Clay Mathematics Institute (CMI) will appoint a special advisory committee to conduct its own final review.

If they give it the green light, the CMI Board of Directors will officially award you the prize.

---

## 2. Number Theory: The Riemann Hypothesis

Often called the "Holy Grail" of pure mathematics, this problem focuses on the predictability of prime numbers.

- **The Concept:** Prime numbers ($2, 3, 5, 7, 11, \dots$) seem to appear randomly. However, Bernhard Riemann discovered that their distribution is deeply connected to a complex function called the **Riemann Zeta Function**.
- **The Question:** The hypothesis states that all the non-obvious inputs that make this function equal zero fall on a single, perfectly straight "critical line" ($x = 1/2$). Proving this would unlock a profound, orderly pattern hidden beneath the apparent chaos of prime numbers.

To claim the $1 million Millennium Prize offered by the Clay Mathematics Institute for the **Riemann Hypothesis**, you must provide a proof that satisfies one specific mathematical condition regarding the Riemann zeta function, denoted as $\zeta(s)$.

Here is precisely what you need to prove, along with the strict administrative rules for submitting it.

---

## 1. The Mathematical Requirement

You must provide a rigorous, flawless mathematical proof showing that **all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function have a real part equal to $1/2$**.

The Riemann zeta function is defined for complex numbers $s = \sigma + it$ (where $\sigma$ and $t$ are real numbers) by the infinite series:

$$\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \frac{1}{1^s} + \frac{1}{2^s} + \frac{1}{3^s} + \dots$$

This series only converges when the real part $\sigma > 1$. However, the function can be analytically continued to the entire complex plane (except for a simple pole at $s = 1$).

- **Trivial Zeros:** It is already known that $\zeta(s) = 0$ when $s$ is a negative even integer ($-2, -4, -6, \dots$). These are called the "trivial zeros."
- **Non-Trivial Zeros:** All other zeros lie within a vertical strip in the complex plane called the **critical strip**, where $0 \le \sigma \le 1$.

**Your Task:** You must prove that every single one of these non-trivial zeros lies exactly on the **critical line**, where the real part is exactly $1/2$. In other words, if $\zeta(s) = 0$ and $s$ is not a negative even integer, then $s = \frac{1}{2} + it$.

---

> **Note on Counterexamples:** If you manage to prove the Riemann Hypothesis is actually _false_ by providing a concrete, verifiable counterexample (a non-trivial zero that does _not_ have a real part of $1/2$), you are still eligible for the $1 million prize, provided the counterexample meets the same publication and waiting-period requirements.

## 3. Fluid Dynamics: Navier-Stokes Existence and Smoothness

This problem bridges the gap between engineering reality and mathematical physics.

- **The Concept:** The Navier-Stokes equations are used every day to simulate airflow over airplane wings, ocean currents, and weather patterns. They work incredibly well in practice.
- **The Question:** Mathematically, we don't actually know if these equations _always_ have smooth, physically reasonable solutions in three dimensions. The challenge is to prove whether solutions will always exist without breaking down into infinite values (singularities) or turbulent chaos that makes the math collapse.

To claim the $1 million Millennium Prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute for the **Navier-Stokes Existence and Smoothness** problem, you must provide a mathematically rigorous proof for one of four statements outlined in the official problem description.

Essentially, you have to prove that physically reasonable solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations _always_ exist and remain well-behaved, **or** you must prove that they can break down.

Here is exactly what you need to deliver to win the prize.

---

## The Equations in Question

The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids (like water or air) move in three-dimensional space. For an incompressible, viscous fluid, the equations are:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + (u \cdot \nabla)u = -\nabla p + \nu \Delta u + f$$

$$\nabla \cdot u = 0$$

Where:

- $u(x, t)$ is the velocity vector field of the fluid.
- $p(x, t)$ is the pressure.
- $\nu > 0$ is the kinematic viscosity.
- $f(x, t)$ is an external force (like gravity).

To win the prize, you must analyze these equations in **three dimensions** ($n = 3$) under physically realistic starting conditions (smooth initial velocity with finite kinetic energy) and prove one of the two main paths: **Acceptance** or **Breakdown**.

---

## Path A: Prove "Global Regularity" (It Always Works)

You must prove that the equations never break down. You can do this in one of two settings:

### 1. In Whole Space ($\mathbb{R}^3$)

You must prove that for any smooth, divergence-free initial velocity field $u_0(x)$ that doesn't blow up at infinity (finite energy), there exist global, smooth solutions for all future time $t \ge 0$.

- **The Catch:** The velocity $u(x,t)$ and pressure $p(x,t)$ must remain completely smooth (differentiable infinitely many times) and their energy must remain bounded for all time.

### 2. In a Periodic Domain ($\mathbb{T}^3$)

Instead of infinite space, you can restrict the problem to a "torus" (a periodic box, meaning if a fluid flows out the right side, it comes back in the left side). You must prove the exact same existence and smoothness conditions apply globally in time within this periodic boundary.

---

## Path B: Prove a "Blow-Up" (It Breaks Down)

Conversely, you can win the prize by proving that the equations _fail_. If you can find even one physically valid starting scenario where the mathematics completely collapses, you claim the prize.

### 3. Breakdown in Whole Space ($\mathbb{R}^3$)

You must construct an example of a smooth initial velocity field $u_0(x)$ with finite energy, and a smooth external force $f(x,t)$, such that **no smooth solution exists for all time**.

- **What breakdown looks like:** Usually, this means showing a "finite-time blow-up"—a point in time $T$ where the fluid velocity or its gradient becomes infinitely large, meaning the equations lose their physical meaning.

### 4. Breakdown in a Periodic Domain ($\mathbb{T}^3$)

Similarly, you can construct a counterexample within a periodic box where a smooth initial state and force inevitably lead to a catastrophic mathematical breakdown or singularity in finite time.

## 4. Quantum Physics & Geometry: Yang-Mills and the Mass Gap

This problem seeks a rigorous mathematical foundation for particle physics.

- **The Concept:** Quantum physics describes the universe using equations that work brilliantly in experiments. One of these frameworks is the Yang-Mills theory, which describes the strong force holding atomic nuclei together.
- **The Question:** Experiments show that the smallest particles have a positive minimum mass (a "mass gap"), explaining why light travels forever but the strong nuclear force has a short range. However, no one has been able to prove _mathematically_ from the core equations why this mass gap must exist.

To claim the **$1 million Millennium Prize** from the Clay Mathematics Institute for the Yang-Mills Existence and Mass Gap problem, you cannot just write down a physics argument or a computer simulation. You must provide a rigorous, watertight **mathematical proof** that fulfills two explicit conditions for quantum Yang-Mills theory in four-dimensional space.

According to the official problem description written by physicists Arthur Jaffe and Edward Witten, here is exactly what your proof must establish:

---

## The Two Core Requirements

### 1. Proof of "Existence"

You must prove that for any compact, simple Lie group $G$ (such as $SU(2)$ or $SU(3)$), a non-trivial quantum Yang-Mills theory actually exists on four-dimensional Euclidean space ($\mathbb{R}^4$).

- **The Catch:** "Existence" means the theory must be established to the standards of contemporary constructive quantum field theory. Specifically, it must satisfy a standard set of axioms, such as the **Wightman axioms** or Osterwalder-Schrader axioms.
- You must show that the mathematical framework can rigorously handle the infinites and ultraviolet divergences that physicists usually sweep under the rug using "renormalization" shortcuts.

### 2. Proof of the "Mass Gap"

You must prove that this specific theory has a **mass gap** ($\Delta > 0$).

- **What this means:** The vacuum state (empty space) has an energy of zero. The mass gap means that the next lowest possible energy state of the theory—the lightest predicted particle—must have a strictly positive mass ($\Delta$).
- There cannot be any massless particles (like photons) in this theory, nor can there be particles with an arbitrarily small, continuous range of masses approaching zero. Every excitation of the field must have a baseline minimum mass.

---

### A Common Misconception to Avoid

Physicists already have mountain-loads of experimental evidence (from particle accelerators) and numerical evidence (from supercomputer "lattice" simulations) showing that a mass gap exists in the real world. However, **physical intuition and computer simulations count as zero proof for this prize.** The challenge is entirely about building the mathematical bridge that proves _why_ it happens from first principles.

## 5. Algebraic Geometry: The Hodge Conjecture

This is a highly abstract geometric problem.

- **The Concept:** Mathematicians study complex geometric shapes (called algebraic varieties) by cutting them up into simpler, interlocking geometric pieces.
- **The Question:** The Hodge Conjecture asserts that for certain nice types of spaces, complex geometric shapes can always be approximated or built out of simpler algebraic pieces. It connects topology (the study of shapes) with algebraic geometry.

To claim the $1 million Millennium Prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute for the Hodge Conjecture, you must provide a rigorous, peer-reviewed mathematical proof (or disproof) of the conjecture that stands up to the scrutiny of the global mathematics community for at least two years.

Here is exactly what you need to prove, what you need to submit, and how the process works.

---

## 1. The Mathematical Requirement: What to Prove

You must prove that for **projective algebraic varieties** (smooth, geometric shapes defined by polynomial equations), certain geometric pieces called **Hodge classes** are actually made up of smaller, simpler geometric pieces called **algebraic cycles** (rational linear combinations of algebraic subvarieties).

In formal mathematical terms, you must show that on a smooth projective variety $X$, the Hodge decomposition interacts with the cohomology groups such that:

$$H^{p,p}(X) \cap H^{2p}(X, \mathbb{Q}) = H^{2p}_{\text{alg}}(X, \mathbb{Q})$$

> **In plain English:** You need to show that complex, abstract topological shapes (which are easy to calculate using calculus) can always be broken down into, or built from, rigid geometric shapes defined strictly by polynomial equations (which are easier to understand algebraically).

---

## Has anyone come close?

Unlike the Poincaré Conjecture (solved by Grigori Perelman in 2003), the Hodge Conjecture remains completely open. Most mathematicians believe it is true, but we currently lack the mathematical tools to prove it generally.

If you think you've found a pathway to the solution, your first step isn't contacting the CMI—it's formatting your proof into a LaTeX manuscript and uploading it to [arXiv.org](https://arxiv.org) for the math world to see.

## 6. Elliptic Curves: The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture

This problem links algebra to geometry through specific types of curves.

- **The Concept:** Elliptic curves look like loops or curves and are defined by equations like $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$. They are incredibly important for modern cryptography (like the security protecting your smartphone).
- **The Question:** The conjecture suggests there is a predictable relationship between the number of rational points (points where both coordinates are fractions) on the curve and a complex calculus-based function linked to it.

To claim the $1 million Millennium Prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute for the **Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) Conjecture**, you cannot just provide a few examples or a partial proof. You have to deliver a definitive mathematical breakthrough.

Here is exactly what you need to provide and the strict process you must follow:

---

## 1. The Mathematical Requirement

You must provide a **complete, rigorous mathematical proof** of the conjecture for all elliptic curves over algebraic number fields (or at least over the rational numbers, $\mathbb{Q}$, as originally stated).

In plain terms, you must prove the exact relationship between two deeply different mathematical objects:

1. **The Algebraic Side:** The rank of the group of rational points on an elliptic curve $E$ (the number of independent infinite solutions to the curve's equation).
2. **The Analytic Side:** The order of vanishing of the Hasse-Weil L-function $L(E, s)$ associated with the curve at the point $s = 1$.

Your proof must show that the rank $r$ is exactly equal to the order of vanishing. If $L(E, 1) = 0$, the rank must be greater than 0 (infinitely many rational points). If $L(E, 1) \neq 0$, the rank must be 0 (finitely many rational points).

---

## What You _Cannot_ Just Provide

- **Proofs for Specific Cases:** Brilliant mathematicians (like Coates, Wiles, Gross, Zagier, and Bhargava) have already proven the conjecture for certain types of elliptic curves (e.g., when the analytic rank is 0 or 1). A prize-winning submission must crack the general case.

---

## The Outlier: Beal's Conjecture ($1 Million)

Outside of the Clay Institute, a Texas banker and math enthusiast named Andrew Beal established a separate **$1 million prize** through the American Mathematical Society for a problem in number theory.

- **The Concept:** You might know Fermat's Last Theorem ($A^n + B^n = C^n$ has no whole-number solutions for $n > 2$). Beal's Conjecture steps it up.
- **The Question:** It states that if $A^X + B^Y = C^Z$, where $A, B, C, X, Y, Z$ are positive integers and $X, Y, Z$ are all greater than 2, then $A, B,$ and $C$ must share a common prime factor. For example, $2^3 + 2^3 = 2^4$ works, but all bases are 2 (sharing a factor). No one has found a counterexample, nor a proof.

To claim the **$1,000,000 Beal Prize** (which is held in trust by the American Mathematical Society), you cannot simply mail a notebook or send an email directly to the committee and ask for the cash. The prize has strict, formal conditions designed to filter out errors.

Here is exactly what you have to provide and achieve to claim it:

### 1. The Core Deliverable (Choose One)

You must provide one of two things:

- **A Proof:** A complete, mathematically sound proof showing that if $A^x + B^y = C^z$ (where $A, B, C, x, y, z$ are positive integers and $x, y, z > 2$), then $A$, $B$, and $C$ must share a common prime factor.
- **A Counterexample:** A specific, verified set of numbers where $x, y, z > 2$ and $A, B, C$ are **coprime** (they share no common prime factors), but the equation $A^x + B^y = C^z$ still holds true.
